Part 5.5 · You are the sensor — and the attacker (phone-sensor lab)

Runs on the MQTT phone-sensor bridge (the QR page). No extra hardware — your own phone is the sensor and the attack tool. Pairs with the Signal Journey view in the Logic Studio toolbar.

The setup. Scan the bridge QR with your phone, connect to the broker, and Share a sensor (light, motion, or orientation — the ones tagged used by logic). The M5StampLC twin subscribes to it and maps it into an input register (%IW) your ladder can read. You are now a field instrument on the plant. Then you will attack the plant you are part of — two different ways.

Attack A — Availability: take the sensor away

With your sensor feeding a rung, press Transmit → OFF. Watch the controller: after the freshness window (~8 s) the point ages out and the logic loses the reading. What does your program do when an input it trusts simply stops — hold last value, fault, fail open, fail closed? Find the answer in your logic, not in the prose.

  • SHOW — the controller state (or io / the point readout) before and after you cut transmission; the stale/aged indication.
  • SAY — what the logic did with the missing input, and whether that is safe.
  • WHY — the real-world consequence of a sensor that drops out, and the design that makes the safe behavior guaranteed rather than accidental (watchdog / last-good timeout / fail-safe default).

Maps to: CWE-1150 / availability failure of a critical function; ATT&CK for ICS T0815 Denial of View, T0814 Denial of Service; defender D3FEND / CIE: liveness checks on every critical input.

Attack B — Integrity: make it lie

Set Override → spoof and drag the slider to a value you choose. Your phone now publishes a value you decided while the plant believes it — false-data injection. Drive the logic across a threshold it guards (e.g. hold a level/tilt below an alarm limit while the real reading is past it) and show the logic making the wrong call on good-looking data.

  • SHOW — the spoofed value on the wire (the bridge log / an MQTT or Wireshark capture of phone/<id>/<sensor>), and the controller acting on it. Note the card reads “SPOOFED 20 (real ≈ 512)” — the phone knows it is lying; the wire does not.
  • SAY — which lenses in the Signal Journey still catch this and which do not. (The byte is on the wire, is in the SRC_WEB/MQTT log, is attributable to your phone — and is still false.)
  • WHY — why “it’s logged and attributable” does not mean “it’s true,” and what independent check would have caught it: a second sensor, a physical limit (float switch / hard stop), a rate-of-change / plausibility gate.

Maps to: CWE-20 (improper input validation), CWE-345 (insufficient verification of data authenticity), CWE-1254 (no plausibility check); ATT&CK for ICS T1692.002 Unauthorized Message: Reporting Message (formerly T0856), T1692.001 Unauthorized Message: Command Message (formerly T0855) (the button path); CAPEC-148 (content spoofing).

Then defend it

Add a plausibility gate to your logic (range + rate-of-change limit, or a cross-check against a second input) so a spoofed value is rejected or flagged instead of acted on — and prove the legitimate reading still works afterward. This is the same move as the Modbus REMOTE-mode gate (CWE-306) one layer up: don’t trust an input just because it arrived.

CYBER-INFORMED ENGINEERING — don’t trust a single sensor. Every value entering control logic is a claim, not a fact. A safety-critical decision needs redundant, independent, or physically-bounded measurement — so that one phone (or one compromised transmitter) cannot talk the plant into the wrong state. Where in your design does one unverified input decide something that matters?

What to submit

  • Attack A and Attack B each with a Show / Say / Why and evidence (captures/screenshots of the wire + the controller).
  • Your plausibility gate: the rung/ST, the spoof it now rejects, and proof the legitimate reading still passes.
  • One paragraph tying it to the Signal Journey: for the spoof, name the lens that fails to catch it and the lens (or independent check) that does.
